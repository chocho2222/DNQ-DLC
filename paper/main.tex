{Dynamic-Neighborhood Quality-Guided World Models for Online Multi-Car Overtaking}

\begin{abstract}
Online multi-car overtaking requires the interaction representation to
change as nearby vehicles become more or less relevant. This paper
presents Dynamic-Neighborhood Quality-Guided Deep Latent Competition
(DNQ-DLC), a world-model planning framework that combines online graph
construction with quality-guided prediction and control. At each real
control step, DNQ-DLC scores map-conditioned traffic relations,
reconstructs an ego-centric interaction graph, and encodes the selected
vehicles in a bounded mask-padded tensor. This design keeps the neural
interface fixed while the represented vehicle identities and traffic
density change. A Graph-DLC world model predicts short-horizon packed
states, reward, boundary risk, and uncertainty. A receding-horizon
objective then selects overtaking actions by balancing progress,
interaction quality, and track adherence. On a 200-case matched
simulator benchmark, DNQ-DLC achieved an 85.5\% overtaking-success rate,
compared with 71.5\% for Rule Expert, corresponding to a paired increase
of 14.0 percentage points. These results support a pass-completion
advantage for the complete controller in the evaluated simulator.
Component tests do not show that every module is individually superior.
Code, data, checkpoints, figures, and simulation videos are available at
\url{https://chocho2222.github.io/DNQ-DLC/}.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Autonomous racing, overtaking, world models, dynamic interaction graphs,

\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{A}{utonomous} racing is a demanding testbed for
intelligent transportation systems. Learning-based agents have reached
champion-level performance in head-to-head simulated car racing
\cite{wurman2022granturismo}, showing the need for fast closed-loop
decision making near vehicle limits. Autonomous-driving evaluation also
faces a scarcity of safety-critical interactions. Naturalistic and
adversarial testing has therefore become important for exposing rare
failures \cite{feng2021intelligenttest,liu2024rarity}.

Overtaking concentrates these challenges into a short time window. The
ego vehicle must find a passing opportunity, act on it, and remain within
the track corridor as nearby competitors change position. The most
relevant opponent can change from a leading vehicle to a lateral or
rear vehicle within one maneuver. Online overtaking is therefore both a
control problem and an interaction-selection problem.

Full-stack racing systems have demonstrated high-speed time-trial and
oval operation \cite{betz2023tum,wischnewski2022iac}. Head-to-head and
multi-vehicle studies extend this setting to changing opponent behavior
\cite{jung2023kaist,raji2022multivehicle}. Tube-based control provides a
further model-based approach to multi-vehicle racing
\cite{wischnewski2023tube}. A survey of autonomous racing summarizes the main system-level
challenges \cite{betz2022autonomous}. Reviews of learned driving discuss
its broader strengths and limitations
\cite{kiran2021drlsurvey,yurtsever2020survey}.
Many learned controllers, however, still use a fixed observation layout
or require retraining when the number and arrangement of surrounding
vehicles change. This is restrictive in multi-car overtaking because the
interaction topology changes online.

World models allow a controller to compare short-horizon futures before
executing an action. This idea supports general latent control
\cite{hafner2023dreamerv3}, model-based driving
\cite{hu2022mile,hu2023gaia}, and Deep Latent Competition (DLC) for
racing \cite{schwarting2021dlc}. A fixed vehicle topology still limits
such models. The planner may retain irrelevant agents or omit the vehicle
that currently constrains the pass. A progress-only objective can also
favor fast but off-track maneuvers.

This paper proposes \method, a dynamic-neighborhood
quality-guided world-model framework for online multi-car overtaking.
At every decision step, the framework projects traffic states into a
map-conditioned representation, reconstructs the interaction graph, and
selects the most relevant surrounding vehicles. The variable physical
neighborhood is converted into a bounded graph tensor and processed by a
Graph-DLC world model for short-horizon prediction. Quality-guided action
evaluation then converts predicted state, reward, boundary risk, and
uncertainty into an overtaking decision. The physical interaction set
can therefore change online without changing the neural input dimension
or binding representation slots to fixed vehicle identities.

The main technical contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose an online dynamic-neighborhood graph construction
    mechanism for multi-car racing. It rebuilds map-conditioned
    interactions from current vehicle states and represents changing
    vehicle identities and counts through a bounded,
    permutation-stable graph tensor.
    \item We develop a dynamic-graph Graph-DLC world model with
    quality-guided short-horizon planning. The model jointly predicts
    future state, reward, boundary risk, and uncertainty, enabling
    overtaking actions to be evaluated as interaction relationships
    evolve.
    \item We conduct extensive closed-loop experiments against
    rule-based, DLC, and multi-seed reinforcement-learning baselines.
    On 200 matched cases, DNQ-DLC achieves an 85.5\% overtaking-success
    rate, exceeding Rule Expert by 14.0 percentage points. Held-out and
    randomized evaluations provide complementary validation across
    multiple tracks and scenarios with 4--8 vehicles.
\end{itemize}
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews
autonomous racing, learned policies, world models, and interaction-aware
representations. Section III presents the dynamic-neighborhood interface,
Graph-DLC world model, and quality-guided planner. Section IV describes
the evaluation protocol and experimental results. Section V discusses
the supported claims and their boundaries, and Section VI concludes the
paper and outlines future real-vehicle validation.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Autonomous racing has evolved into a high-speed full-stack benchmark
for intelligent transportation systems. The survey by Betz
et al.~\cite{betz2022autonomous} characterizes autonomous vehicle racing as
a domain that simultaneously stresses perception, planning, control,
vehicle dynamics, and system integration. System-level studies such as
the TUM autonomous motorsport stack and the KAIST IAC system further
show that head-to-head racing requires reliable real-time decision
making under changing opponent behavior~\cite{betz2023tum,jung2023kaist}.
More recent A2RL work demonstrates that autonomous racing continues to
move toward full-scale multi-agent operation at the limits of vehicle
handling~\cite{hoffmann2026a2rl}. These racing systems motivate the
setting of this paper, but they also reveal a central difficulty: dense
multi-car overtaking requires the planner not only to control the ego
vehicle, but also to decide which surrounding vehicles should influence
the next decision.

Optimization-based racing planners provide one way to address this
problem when the interaction structure is known or can be specified
compactly. Multi-vehicle racing planners and tube-MPC formulations have
shown strong performance in high-speed racing scenarios
\cite{raji2022multivehicle,wischnewski2023tube}. Their strength lies in
systematic optimization and constraint handling, but their performance
depends on the quality of the assumed vehicle model, opponent model, and
interaction set. In dense overtaking, the relevant opponents can change
within a few decision steps: a forward vehicle may become irrelevant
after the ego vehicle initiates a pass, while a lateral or rear vehicle
may become the dominant constraint. This motivates a representation that
selects the interaction set online rather than keeping it fixed.

Learned policies provide another route to adaptive racing behavior.
Deep reinforcement learning has demonstrated highly competitive racing
performance in high-fidelity game environments~\cite{wurman2022granturismo}, and
recent F1TENTH work shows that overtaking behaviors can be learned and
transferred to a physical small-scale racing platform
\cite{steiner2025f1tenth_overtaking}. Surveys of deep reinforcement
learning for autonomous driving highlight the potential of learned
policies for decision making under complex dynamics, but also point out
their sensitivity to state representation, training distribution, and
reward design~\cite{kiran2021drlsurvey}. This limitation is especially
important for online multi-car overtaking. A policy trained with predetermined
observation semantics may implicitly associate a particular input slot
with a particular opponent role, even though the physical vehicle
occupying that role may change during the maneuver. DNQ-DLC therefore
separates proposal generation from interaction selection: learned actors
consume a bounded mask-padded tensorization, while the
dynamic-neighborhood module rebuilds the physical interaction graph and
determines which vehicles are represented at the current decision step.

The limitations of direct policy execution motivate predictive planners
that can compare candidate actions before committing to one. World
models learn dynamics that support planning in imagined futures
\cite{hafner2023dreamerv3}. In autonomous driving, MILE connects
model-based imitation learning with closed-loop urban driving
\cite{hu2022mile}, while GAIA-1 demonstrates that generative world
models can represent complex driving-scene evolution~\cite{hu2023gaia}.
Recent driving world-model surveys summarize this direction as a
promising way to model scene evolution and support interaction with
dynamic environments~\cite{tu2025dwm_survey}. More recent
planning-oriented world-model methods further emphasize that learned
dynamics should be aligned with downstream planning objectives:
AdaWM studies adaptive world-model planning under distribution shift
\cite{wang2025adawm}, WorldRFT uses reinforcement fine-tuning to align
latent world-model representations with safety-critical planning
behavior~\cite{yang2026worldrft}, and VLA-World combines predictive
imagination with reflective reasoning for autonomous-driving
foresight~\cite{wang2026vlaworld}.

These world-model studies show the value of prediction for decision
making, but autonomous racing imposes a sharper requirement. The planner
must decide whether an overtaking attempt is desirable under short
horizons, narrow lateral margins, and close interactions. A rollout that
maximizes progress alone may still produce off-track exposure or unsafe
side-by-side behavior. Therefore, DNQ-DLC uses the world model for
candidate action evaluation rather than passive forecasting. Candidate
rollouts are ranked by overtaking progress, track adherence, lateral
quality, close-gap risk, predicted boundary risk, rollout uncertainty,
and consistency with learned actor priors.

For world-model planning to be effective in traffic, the model must
receive an interaction representation that remains valid as the number
and relevance of nearby agents change. A recent survey of autonomous
vehicle motion forecasting emphasizes that predicting the future
behavior of surrounding traffic participants is essential for downstream
decision making~\cite{shi2025motionforecasting}. Large-scale datasets
such as Waymo Open Motion and Argoverse 2 provide benchmarks for
interactive multi-agent prediction~\cite{ettinger2021waymo,
wilson2023argoverse2}. Transformer-based forecasting models such as
Scene Transformer and Wayformer show that attention-based interaction
modeling can capture dependencies among multiple agents
\cite{ngiam2021scene,nayakanti2023wayformer}. Map-relative and
vectorized representations also play an important role: DenseTNT
formulates trajectory prediction through dense goal sets, and HiVT uses
hierarchical vectorized interaction modeling for multi-agent motion
prediction~\cite{gu2021densetnt,zhou2022hivt}.

Most of these interaction models target forecasting or urban traffic
prediction rather than online racing control. Related game-theoretic
and traffic-generation methods further emphasize the importance of
explicit multi-agent relations, as seen in GameFormer and
TrafficGen~\cite{huang2023gameformer,feng2023trafficgen}. DNQ-DLC
adapts these interaction-modeling ideas to a receding-horizon racing
controller. At each decision step, the method rebuilds a map-relative
ego-centered graph, tensorizes the currently relevant vehicles into a
bounded masked observation, and uses this runtime graph representation
inside a world-model planner. Thus, the graph is not only a forecasting
input; it determines which vehicles are imagined, scored, and acted upon
in the next overtaking decision.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section presents DNQ-DLC, a dynamic-neighborhood
quality-guided world-model planner for online multi-car overtaking in
autonomous racing. DNQ-DLC operates as a receding-horizon decision layer
above the low-level vehicle dynamics controller. At each decision step,
it constructs a map-conditioned dynamic-neighborhood graph, packs the
selected interaction-relevant vehicles into a bounded mask-padded graph
tensor, predicts short-horizon futures under candidate controls, and
executes the first action of the highest-scoring rollout.

The method contains three main computational stages after problem
formulation: dynamic map-conditioned neighborhood encoding,
Graph-DLC world modeling, and quality-guided receding-horizon planning.
The packed-observation layout, self-feature definition, network
architecture, and numerical configuration are detailed in Supplementary
Section I. Training labels, losses, and baseline budgets are reported in
Supplementary Section II. Candidate generation and scoring are specified
in Supplementary Section III, and metric definitions and experimental
protocols are defined in Supplementary Section IV.

\textit{Notation convention.}
Subscript $t$ denotes the physical decision time. Superscript $q$
denotes an arbitrary vehicle when defining local geometric quantities,
superscript $j$ denotes a surrounding vehicle in an ego-neighbor
relation, superscript $i$ denotes an agent-centered row, superscript
$k$ denotes a packed relation slot, and superscript $(n)$ denotes a
candidate rollout. A hatted variable denotes an imagined quantity
predicted by the world model. The notation $t+k|t$ denotes a $k$-step
prediction made from decision time $t$. Variables without row
superscript refer to the controlled ego row; for example,
$z_t\equiv z_t^0$ and $h_t\equiv h_t^0$.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{subsec:problem}

At decision time $t$, the ego vehicle observes a track map
$\mathcal{M}$, its own state $x_t^0$, and a set of surrounding vehicles
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I}_t=\{1,2,\ldots,N_t^{\mathrm{veh}}\}.
\end{equation}
The ego state includes position, velocity, heading, track-relative
progress, lateral deviation, and driving-status indicators. Surrounding
vehicles are represented by the same physical quantities when
available. State normalization is specified in Supplementary Section I-B and
Table SII. The frozen numerical configuration is listed in
Supplementary Section I-G and Table SIV.

The controller outputs a normalized low-level racing action
\begin{equation}
a_t=[\delta_t,u_t,b_t]^\top\in\mathcal{A},
\end{equation}
where $\delta_t$, $u_t$, and $b_t$ denote steering, throttle, and brake.
The admissible action set is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}
=
[\delta_{\min},\delta_{\max}]
\times
[u_{\min},u_{\max}]
\times
[b_{\min},b_{\max}].
\end{equation}

For each candidate first action $a_t^{(n)}$, DNQ-DLC forms a
short-horizon action continuation
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}_t^{(n)}
=
(a_t^{(n)},\hat a_{t+1|t}^{(n)},\ldots,
\hat a_{t+H-1|t}^{(n)}),
\end{equation}
evaluates the imagined future with a multi-objective score
$J(\mathbf{A}_t^{(n)})$, and executes only the first action. Candidate
generation and scoring are described in Section~\ref{subsec:planning}.

\subsection{Dynamic Map-Conditioned Neighborhood Encoding}
\label{subsec:graph}

Dense racing traffic changes rapidly. A predetermined vehicle ordering may
include irrelevant vehicles or miss the currently decisive opponent.
DNQ-DLC therefore selects interaction-relevant vehicles online,
rebuilds the local interaction graph, and tensorizes that graph through
bounded masked relation slots.

\textit{Track-conditioned self geometry.}
For any vehicle $q\in\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}_t$, let
$c(\eta_t^q)$ be the closest centerline point to the vehicle, and let
$\hat{\mathbf t}(\eta_t^q)$ and $\hat{\mathbf n}(\eta_t^q)$ be the
corresponding local tangent and normal unit vectors. The track-relative
self geometry is
\begin{equation}
\Delta x_{c,t}^q
=
p_{x,t}^q-c_x(\eta_t^q),
\qquad
\Delta y_{c,t}^q
=
p_{y,t}^q-c_y(\eta_t^q),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\ell_t^q
=
\frac{
\langle p_t^q-c(\eta_t^q),\hat{\mathbf n}(\eta_t^q)\rangle
}{w_t^q+\epsilon},
\qquad
e_{\psi,t}^q
=
\mathrm{wrap}(\psi_c(\eta_t^q)-\psi_t^q).
\end{equation}
This sign convention follows the submitted telemetry builder, where the
heading-error features are represented by
$\sin(\psi_c(\eta_t^q)-\psi_t^q)$ and
$\cos(\psi_c(\eta_t^q)-\psi_t^q)$. The self feature block
$\phi_t^q$ combines these track-conditioned quantities with normalized
global position, velocity, progress, and driving-status flags. Its exact
layout is reported in Supplementary Section I-D and Table SII.

For the controlled ego row, we also define the local map context
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_t^0
=
\{c(\eta_t^0),\hat{\mathbf t}(\eta_t^0),
\hat{\mathbf n}(\eta_t^0),w_t^0,\kappa_t^0\},
\end{equation}
where $w_t^0$ is the local track-width scale and $\kappa_t^0$ is a
look-ahead curvature feature.

\textit{Map-conditioned relation and interaction selection.}
For each surrounding vehicle $j\in\mathcal{I}_t$, DNQ-DLC forms the
ego-neighbor physical relation vector
\begin{equation}
r_t^{0j}
=
[
\Delta f_{b,t}^{0j},
\Delta l_{b,t}^{0j},
\Delta v_{x,t}^{0j},
\Delta v_{y,t}^{0j},
d_t^j,
\sin(\Delta\psi_t^{0j}),
\cos(\Delta\psi_t^{0j})
]^\top .
\end{equation}
Here $\Delta f_{b,t}^{0j}$ and $\Delta l_{b,t}^{0j}$ are the
ego-body forward and left displacements used by the submitted telemetry
builder, $\Delta v_{x,t}^{0j}$ and $\Delta v_{y,t}^{0j}$ are normalized
global velocity differences, $d_t^j$ is the normalized ego-neighbor
distance, and $\Delta\psi_t^{0j}=\psi_t^j-\psi_t^0$ is the pairwise
heading difference. Thus, the relation slot is map-conditioned through
the ego state and local map context, but it is not a purely
centerline-frame relation in every channel.

The interaction relevance score is written abstractly as
\begin{equation}
\rho_j(t)
=
\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{int}}(r_t^{0j},\mathcal{F}_t^0),
\end{equation}
and the eligible local neighborhood is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}_t
=
\mathrm{Gate}_{\mathrm{int}}
(
\mathcal{I}_t,
\{\rho_j(t)\}_j,
\mathcal{F}_t^0
).
\end{equation}
All reported experiments use the implemented runtime interaction
selector specified in Supplementary Section I-F (Runtime Interaction
Selector). The
abstract $\mathrm{Score}_{\mathrm{int}}$ and
$\mathrm{Gate}_{\mathrm{int}}$ notation is used to describe the general
role of relevance-based interaction selection in the method pipeline.

\textit{Bounded graph tensorization and validity mask.}
The selected ego-centric graph is denoted by
\begin{equation}
G_t^0=(V_t^0,\mathcal{E}_t^0,\mathbf m_t^0),
\end{equation}
where $V_t^0$ contains the controlled ego vehicle and selected
neighbors, $\mathcal{E}_t^0$ contains ego-neighbor relation edges, and
$\mathbf m_t^0$ is the deterministic validity mask generated by
bounded slot tensorization. Let $K_{\max}$ denote the relation capacity
used to batch the runtime graph. Vehicles in $\mathcal{N}_t$ are sorted by
$\rho_j(t)$, and
\begin{equation}
j_t^k=\mathrm{TopK}_k(\mathcal{N}_t;\rho),
\qquad
k=1,\ldots,K_{\max},
\end{equation}
denotes the $k$-th highest-ranked valid neighbor when it exists. The
validity mask is
\begin{equation}
m_t^{0,k}
=
\mathbb{I}
[
k\le\min(|\mathcal{N}_t|,K_{\max})
],
\end{equation}
and the packed physical relation is
\begin{equation}
\tilde r_{\mathrm{phy},t}^{0,k}
=
\begin{cases}
r_t^{0j_t^k}, & m_t^{0,k}=1,\\
\mathbf{0}, & m_t^{0,k}=0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Thus,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf m_t^0=\{m_t^{0,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{\max}},
\qquad
y_t^{0,k}=[\tilde r_{\mathrm{phy},t}^{0,k},m_t^{0,k}].
\end{equation}
The resulting bounded tensor representation of the runtime graph is
\begin{equation}
\bar{o}_t
=
\mathrm{Pack}(G_t^0,\mathcal{M})
=
[
\phi_t^0,
y_t^{0,1},
\ldots,
y_t^{0,K_{\max}}
].
\end{equation}
Padded slots contain zero physical relation features and have zero
validity mask. When $|\mathcal{N}_t|>K_{\max}$, only the $K_{\max}$
highest-ranked vehicles are represented by the network at that
decision step. Thus, ``dynamic neighborhood'' means that the physical
interaction graph and represented vehicle identities are rebuilt online;
bounded masked tensorization is used for computation and is not a
predetermined vehicle topology. The submitted slot capacity and exact
packed-observation layout are given in Supplementary Section I-B
(Packed Observation Layout).

\textit{Graph-context encoding.}
For row $i$, the self feature block
$\phi_t^i\in\mathbb{R}^{d_\phi}$ is encoded as
\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{self},t}^i
=
\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{ego}}(\phi_t^i).
\end{equation}
The physical part of the $k$-th relation slot is encoded as
\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{i,k}
=
\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{rel}}(\tilde r_{\mathrm{phy},t}^{i,k}).
\end{equation}
The validity mask is used for masked attention:
\begin{equation}
b_t^{i,k}
=
\mathrm{Linear}_{\mathrm{att}}(e_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{i,k}),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\alpha_t^{i,k}
=
\frac{
m_t^{i,k}\exp(b_t^{i,k})
}{
\epsilon+\sum_{\ell=1}^{K_{\max}}
m_t^{i,\ell}\exp(b_t^{i,\ell})
}.
\end{equation}
The relation context is
\begin{equation}
\bar e_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{i}
=
\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\max}}
\alpha_t^{i,k}e_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{i,k}.
\end{equation}
The graph-context embedding for row $i$ is
\begin{equation}
z_t^i
=
[
e_{\mathrm{self},t}^{i},
\bar e_{\mathrm{rel},t}^{i}
].
\end{equation}
For the controlled ego row, $z_t\equiv z_t^0$. Thus, $\bar{o}_t$ is the
pre-encoding packed observation, whereas $z_t$ is the encoded
graph-context representation used by the world model.

\subsection{Graph-DLC World Model}
\label{subsec:world_model}

The Graph-DLC world model predicts short-horizon consequences under
candidate actions. Its current observation input is $\bar{o}_t$, which
is encoded into $z_t$, and its control input is a candidate action
$a_t$. The action is encoded as
\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{act},t}
=
\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{act}}(a_t).
\end{equation}

The implementation evaluates valid agent-centered rows in a shared
batch. Let
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}_t=\{0\}\cup\mathcal{I}_t
\end{equation}
be the row set, where row $0$ corresponds to the controlled ego
vehicle. For row $i$, the row-wise action embedding is
\begin{equation}
e_{\mathrm{act},t}^i
=
\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{act}}(a_t^i).
\end{equation}
The global self and action summaries are
\begin{equation}
\bar e_{\mathrm{self},t}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_t|}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{R}_t}
e_{\mathrm{self},t}^i,
\qquad
\bar e_{\mathrm{act},t}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_t|}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{R}_t}
e_{\mathrm{act},t}^i.
\end{equation}
The fusion input and transition hidden state are
\begin{equation}
\Phi_t^i
=
[
z_t^i,
e_{\mathrm{act},t}^i,
\bar e_{\mathrm{self},t},
\bar e_{\mathrm{act},t}
],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
h_t^i
=
\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{fusion}}(\Phi_t^i).
\end{equation}
Since $z_t^i=[e_{\mathrm{self},t}^i,\bar e_{\mathrm{rel},t}^i]$, this is
equivalent to the five-summary input
$[e_{\mathrm{self},t}^i,\bar e_{\mathrm{rel},t}^i,
e_{\mathrm{act},t}^i,\bar e_{\mathrm{self},t},\bar e_{\mathrm{act},t}]$.
In the decision layer, $h_t\equiv h_t^0$. The treatment of
non-controlled row actions is specified in Supplementary Section I-F
(Runtime Interaction Selector).

DNQ-DLC keeps a DLC-style latent interface:
\begin{equation}
p_\theta(s_t|h_t)
=
\mathcal{N}
(
\mu_t^p,
\mathrm{diag}((\sigma_t^p)^2)
),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
q_\phi(s_t|h_t,\bar{o}_{t+1})
=
\mathcal{N}
(
\mu_t^q,
\mathrm{diag}((\sigma_t^q)^2)
).
\end{equation}
The posterior uses $\bar{o}_{t+1}$ only during training. During online
candidate ranking, the submitted checkpoint uses deterministic mean
rollouts and the deterministic latent state
\begin{equation}
s_t^{\mathrm{eval}}=\mu_t^p .
\end{equation}
Therefore, the uncertainty penalty is derived from the
observation and reward log-variance heads rather than from
KL-regularized latent sampling.

The prediction heads operate on
\begin{equation}
\xi_t=[h_t,s_t].
\end{equation}
They are implemented as linear heads:
\begin{equation}
(\mu_{t+1|t}^{o},\lambda_{t+1|t}^{o})
=
\mathrm{Linear}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\xi_t),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(\mu_{t+1|t}^{r},\lambda_{t+1|t}^{r})
=
\mathrm{Linear}_{\mathrm{rew}}(\xi_t),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
z_{t+1|t}^{\chi}
=
\mathrm{Linear}_{\mathrm{risk}}(\xi_t),
\qquad
\hat{\chi}_{t+1|t}
=
\sigma(z_{t+1|t}^{\chi}).
\end{equation}
Here $\mu_{t+1|t}^{o}$ and $\lambda_{t+1|t}^{o}$ parameterize the next
packed-observation distribution, $\mu_{t+1|t}^{r}$ and
$\lambda_{t+1|t}^{r}$ parameterize the reward distribution, and
$\hat{\chi}_{t+1|t}$ is a boundary-risk probability. The implementation also contains an auxiliary quality head. Its loss
weight is zero in the submitted checkpoint, so the head does not
contribute to quality guidance (Supplementary Section II-C).

The general world-model objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{wm}}
=
\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}
[
&
\mathcal{L}_{o}
+
\mathcal{L}_{r}
+
\lambda_{\chi}
\mathrm{BCE}(\chi_{t+1},\hat{\chi}_{t+1|t})
+
\lambda_q\mathcal{L}_q
]
\\
&
+
\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi\|p_\theta).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In the submitted checkpoint, $\lambda_q=0$ and
$\beta_{\mathrm{KL}}=0$. Therefore, the auxiliary quality loss and KL
regularization are inactive in world-model training.
Quality guidance in the planner comes from the separately
trained quality-biased actor, overtaking and recovery templates, and
online safety-efficiency scoring terms. The Gaussian likelihoods,
dataset size and validation splits are reported in Supplementary
Section II-A. The loss definitions and checkpoint-specific interpretation
are given in Supplementary Section II-C, and actor training is detailed
in Supplementary Section II-D.

\subsection{Quality-Guided Receding-Horizon Planning}
\label{subsec:planning}

DNQ-DLC converts prediction into control by combining learned actor
proposals, analytic overtaking templates, recovery actions, safety
actions, and local perturbations. The analytic templates and recovery
guard are not claimed as learned modules; they serve as structured
racing priors that expand the candidate set and prevent degenerate
learned proposals under boundary or recovery conditions. Before
candidate scoring, a deterministic recovery guard is evaluated. If the
guard is active, DNQ-DLC executes the recovery action directly;
otherwise it ranks candidate rollouts by the score below. Candidate-pool
construction, duplicate removal, passing templates, braking templates,
and the recovery guard are reported in Supplementary Section III
(Candidate Generation and Scoring).

The learned proposals are
\begin{equation}
a_t^{\mathrm{base}}
=
\mathrm{ActorMLP}_{\mathrm{base}}(\bar{o}_t),
\qquad
a_t^{\mathrm{qual}}
=
\mathrm{ActorMLP}_{\mathrm{qual}}(\bar{o}_t).
\end{equation}
Thus, $\bar{o}_t$ serves three roles: it is encoded into graph context
$z_t$, consumed by the proposal actors, and recursively used as the
state interface during imagined rollout.

Analytic passing proposals are activated when the local geometry admits
an attack:
\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{pass}}
=
\mathbb{I}
[
|\ell_t^0|<\ell_{\mathrm{att}},
\ 
\cos(e_{\psi,t}^0)>\eta_{\mathrm{att}},
\ 
n_{\mathrm{front}}+n_{\mathrm{side}}>0
].
\end{equation}
When activated, left- and right-side passing actions are generated as
\begin{equation}
a_t^{\mathrm{pass}}(s,\Delta)
=
[
\mathrm{clip}
(s\Delta-\lambda_\psi e_{\psi,t}^0,\delta_{\min},\delta_{\max}),
\ 
u_{\mathrm{pass}}(\kappa_t^0),
\ 
0
]^\top .
\end{equation}
The concrete trigger values and template constants are provided in
Supplementary Section III (Candidate Generation and Scoring).

For candidate $n$, the imagined rollout starts from
\begin{equation}
\hat{\bar{o}}_{t|t}^{(n)}=\bar{o}_t,
\qquad
\hat a_{t|t}^{(n)}=a_t^{(n)}.
\end{equation}
For future imagined steps $k>0$, the continuation action is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat a_{t+k|t}^{(n)}
=
&
(1-\alpha_q)
\mathrm{ActorMLP}_{\mathrm{base}}
(
\hat{\bar{o}}_{t+k|t}^{(n)}
)
\\
&
+
\alpha_q
\mathrm{ActorMLP}_{\mathrm{qual}}
(
\hat{\bar{o}}_{t+k|t}^{(n)}
).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The same Graph-DLC pipeline is applied to
$\hat{\bar{o}}_{t+k|t}^{(n)}$ and $\hat a_{t+k|t}^{(n)}$, and the next
imagined packed observation is updated by
\begin{equation}
\hat{\bar{o}}_{t+k+1|t}^{(n)}
=
\mathrm{PostPack}
(
\mu_{t+k+1|t}^{o,(n)},
\hat{\mathbf m}_{t+k|t}^{0,(n)}
).
\end{equation}
In the submitted short-horizon rollout, relation-slot identities are
not explicitly tracked as persistent physical agents. The predicted
mean provides continuous self and relation features, while the validity
mask channels are post-processed by $\mathrm{PostPack}(\cdot)$ for the
next masked aggregation. At the next real simulator step, the
neighborhood is rebuilt from telemetry and repacked by
$\mathrm{Pack}(\cdot)$. Implementation details for
$\mathrm{PostPack}(\cdot)$ are provided in Supplementary
Section III-A (Mask Handling During Imagined Rollout).

Each candidate rollout is scored by
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
J(\mathbf{A}_t^{(n)})
=
\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\gamma^k
[
&
\lambda_{\mathrm{prog}}R_{\mathrm{prog}}^{(n,k)}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{ovt}}R_{\mathrm{ovt}}^{(n,k)}
\\
&
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{off}}C_{\mathrm{off}}^{(n,k)}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{lat}}C_{\mathrm{lat}}^{(n,k)}
\\
&
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{close}}C_{\mathrm{close}}^{(n,k)}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{risk}}C_{\mathrm{risk}}^{(n,k)}
\\
&
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{unc}}C_{\mathrm{unc}}^{(n,k)}
-
\lambda_{\mathrm{imit}}C_{\mathrm{imit}}^{(n,k)}
].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Full scoring definitions are given in Supplementary Section III-B
(Scoring Terms).

The selected action is
\begin{equation}
n^\star=\arg\max_n J(\mathbf{A}_t^{(n)}),
\qquad
a_t^\star=a_t^{(n^\star)}.
\end{equation}
DNQ-DLC applies $a_t^\star$, observes telemetry at $t+1$, rebuilds
$G_{t+1}^0$ and $\bar{o}_{t+1}$, and replans. Since this layer uses
weighted penalties rather than formal hard constraints, we describe it
as safety-aware multi-objective scoring rather than strict
safety-constrained optimization.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Metrics}

The primary benchmark contains 200 matched cases per algorithm over
$N\in\{4,5,6,7,8\}$ vehicles. The $N=4$--6 cases match the training
vehicle-count range, whereas $N=7$--8 tests vehicle-count extrapolation.
Frozen-checkpoint validation evaluates the world model and quality actor
on episode-disjoint procedural, Hairpin, and S-Curve data. The component
study uses matched randomized cases in which start slots and vehicle
spacing change. Every policy observes all vehicles and selects $K=3$
neighbors; failed cases are retained.

The benchmark compares \method with Rule Expert, Safety Rule, PPO,
SAC, TD3 \cite{schulman2017ppo,haarnoja2018sac,fujimoto2018td3}, and
three DLC variants. Rule Expert combines lane following, adaptive
driving, and passing modes. Safety Rule uses more conservative barrier
and traffic-density thresholds. Their implementation is described in
Supplementary Section II-B.

DLC-IT, DLC-JT, and DLC-JTO denote the individual-transition,
joint-transition, and joint-transition-plus-observer variants of
Schwarting \etal~\cite{schwarting2021dlc}. We retrained each variant
from four seeds using 120 episodes, 96000 transitions, and 80 updates.
A frozen checkpoint was chosen by a predefined round-robin validation
rule. These baselines use fixed but non-identical training budgets and
architectures. Table~\ref{tab:dlc_multicheckpoint} tests all four final
DLC-JTO checkpoints on eight new matched cases.

The legacy PPO, SAC, and TD3 checkpoints use one seed and 50k environment
steps. The training-budget analysis adds three 200k-step seeds for each
RL family and evaluates them on 12 new randomized $N=4$ cases. Full
training budgets and checkpoint rules are given in Supplementary
Section II-B.

The analysis designates binary overtaking success as the primary
closed-loop endpoint. On-track and desirable overtaking, rank gain,
target grass exposure, the contact proxy, and conditional completion
time are secondary endpoints. Episode-level on-track and desirable
proportions use all cases, with zero assigned when a case has no
completed overtake. Completion time and target-grass summaries are
conditioned on successful cases and always report their sample counts.

Because every algorithm was evaluated on the same 200 case identifiers,
primary contrasts use the paired success-rate difference. We report a
50,000-resample paired case-bootstrap 95\% confidence interval and a
two-sided exact McNemar test. Holm adjustment covers the six reported
baseline contrasts. Absolute success rates retain Wilson intervals for
scale, and vehicle-count strata are reported as sensitivity
analyses rather than independent replications. Model validation reports
state RMSE, reward MAE, risk AUROC, risk AUPRC, Brier score, expected
calibration error, and $H$-step state RMSE. Metric definitions and
statistical summaries are given in Supplementary Section IV-A. The matched primary and randomized attribution protocols are described
in Supplementary Sections IV-B and IV-C, respectively.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The main result is that the complete \method controller improves
overtaking success relative to Rule Expert, Safety Rule, and the
evaluated DLC and legacy RL baselines. This result applies to the full
controller. It does not show that the dynamic selector alone caused the
increase. Overtaking success measures pass completion, not overall
safety. Rule Expert still has lower grass exposure and a higher
desirable-overtaking rate within $N=4$--6.

The supporting experiments explain this result more clearly. The frozen
world model ranks short-horizon boundary risk well, but its prediction
error increases on unseen $N=8$ tracks. The quality actor works in the
procedural training range but does not transfer to the two unseen
$N=8$ tracks. Randomized ablations support several quality-related
terms, whereas the dynamic-selector intervals cross zero. Longer RL
training also improves several baselines, and the methods occupy
different parts of the completion--desirable--grass trade-off. DNQ-DLC
has the highest completion in the 12-case $N=4$ study, but it does not
simultaneously provide the best desirable rate, grass rate, and rank
gain.

The framework also clarifies the role of structured priors. Rule Expert
is both a strong comparator and an internal dependency of the deployed
hybrid planner through the anchor action. Handcrafted candidate
templates, speed limits, and recovery triggers provide additional
inductive bias. The system should therefore be interpreted as learned
short-horizon ranking around structured racing priors, rather than as a
fully learned replacement for rule-based control.

The evidence remains confined to simulation and does not establish
real-vehicle safety, physical collision avoidance, tire-limit validity,
or robustness to actuator delay. The implemented risk target is derived
from off-track, backward-motion, and lateral-error telemetry rather than
physical collision labels. The controller also assumes map-relative
telemetry, leaving perception error, occlusion, localization drift, and
asynchronous sensing outside the evaluated setting. Within imagined
rollouts, relation slots remain fixed; the physical graph is rebuilt
only after the next real simulator observation. The legacy E2/E3 sweeps
are therefore retained as deterministic functional checks rather than
independent scenario evidence.

Training-budget and opponent diversity also constrain comparison. The
additional RL study covers three seeds, 200k steps, $N=4$, and 12 final
checkpoint evaluations without dense learning curves. The DLC suite
uses four training seeds but a lower optimization budget
than DNQ-DLC. The quality actor is trained from 24 filtered episodes and
does not beat the mean-action predictor on two unseen $N=8$ tracks.
Moreover, the opponent set excludes adversarial blocking and human
racecraft. Supplementary Section VI discusses these evidence limits.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced \method, a hybrid world-model planner for
online multi-car overtaking. At each real control step, the method
reconstructs a map-conditioned interaction graph, selects the currently
relevant neighboring vehicles, and packs them into a bounded masked
graph tensor. A Graph-DLC model then predicts short-horizon outcomes,
while a quality-guided receding-horizon planner ranks learned and
structured action candidates. This design separates the changing
physical interaction set from the fixed-dimensional neural interface.

On the 200-case matched benchmark, the complete controller achieved
85.5\% overtaking success, compared with 71.5\% for Rule Expert. The
paired improvement was 14.0 percentage points (95\% CI,
6.5--21.5 percentage points). The advantage was also observed in the
tested $N=7$--8 vehicle-count extrapolation stratum. Frozen-model
validation supports short-horizon boundary-risk ranking, and randomized
attribution supports contributions from risk and safety-quality scoring.
However, the method did not dominate every secondary endpoint, and the
dynamic selector's independent performance advantage was not established.

Future work will move the framework from telemetry-based simulation to
closed-course real-vehicle overtaking experiments. The controller will
be integrated with onboard perception, multi-vehicle tracking,
localization, and a supervisory safety layer, and its latency and
robustness will be evaluated under sensor noise and actuator delay.
Hardware-in-the-loop tests will precede progressively more demanding
multi-vehicle track experiments.
